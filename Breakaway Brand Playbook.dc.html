02

Principles

Four ideas sit behind every rule in this playbook. Learn these four and most of the rules stop needing to be looked up — you will already know what the answer is going to be.

01
Red is the loudest thing you have

So only use it once. Put it on the thing you want people to see first — the score, the date, the button — and let white space do the rest. Red everywhere means red nowhere.

02
Loud type, calm layout

Our headline font is aggressive on purpose. Everything around it stays quiet and lined up — that contrast is what makes us look fast instead of messy.

03
Say one thing

One post, one message. If you are trying to fit a score, a thank-you, and a schedule on the same graphic, you actually have three posts. Empty space is not wasted space.

04
Copy the recipe, don't reinvent it

The same red, the same two fonts, the same rounded corners — in a post, on a shirt, in a slide deck. Pull the values from this page instead of picking new ones, and everything you make looks like it belongs together.

Logo

LockedPick one

Nothing on this page is your call. The artwork is not shown here on purpose — a logo copied out of a document is how wrong versions spread. Check with Tao for the most up to date logos, then follow the rules below exactly.

Rule 0 · where the file comes from
Check with Tao for the most up to date logos.

Every other rule here assumes you started from a current, official file. Ask Tao before you place a logo on anything that leaves the team — a post, a shirt, a banner, a sponsor deck, a website. It takes one message and it is the difference between a clean mark and a stretched screenshot of a 2019 export.

Never source a logo from
a Google Slides deck, a past Instagram post, the website, a t-shirt photo, an image search, or your own downloads folder from last season.
Always source it from
the current official file set — confirmed with Tao. Ask for vector (.ai / .svg / .eps) for anything printed, embroidered, cut, or larger than a screen.
If you are unsure it is current
stop and ask. A file with a year in the name is not proof it is the live one. Colour has changed before and will change again.
Pick the treatment by ground
There are four official treatments and the ground decides which one you take, not taste:
White or N50 ground → primary lockup, red on white.
Carbon, red, or a photo → the reversed white treatment.
Square or tight space → the stacked lockup.
The team number is already on the graphic → the wordmark alone.
Never two treatments on one graphic, and never the wordmark and the full lockup together.
Placement
One logo per graphic, in a corner, sitting on a quiet area. It is a signature, not the message — it never leads a layout and never sits centred above a headline. On a photo it goes on flat, even tone; if the only clear area is busy, put it on a solid panel instead of shadowing or outlining the mark. Keep it fully inside the safe margin (§11).
The logo is not a device
It is never a watermark, a background pattern, a bullet point, a frame, a texture, or a letter inside a word. The Trace never touches it, never points at it, and never runs behind it. If a layout feels like it needs a bigger logo, it usually needs a bigger headline.
Space & size
Clear space on all sides ≥ the height of the round “b” badge — no type, no photo edge, no Trace inside it. Minimum 120px / 1.0in wide for the lockup; 24px floor for the badge alone; ≥ 1in wide in print. Avatars and favicons use the badge, white on a red circle. Below the floor, drop the logo rather than shrinking it.
Never
Recolor · gradient · glow, shadow, or outline · stretch, skew, rotate · re-typeset · box it in · redraw or screenshot it · crop or cut any part of it · animate it apart · add a word or year to the lockup · place it on a busy photo · sit it on red when it is the red version. Any of these and it stops being our mark.
Asking for the right file
Tell Tao three things and you will get the correct export first time: which treatment (primary, reversed, stacked, wordmark), where it is going (Instagram, shirt, banner, deck, website), and how wide it will print or display. Screen use takes a transparent PNG or SVG; anything printed, cut, or embroidered takes vector. Never rescale a small PNG up, never re-save a JPEG of a logo, and never redraw it in Canva.

Rounded corners — how round, and when

Pick one

Any time you draw a box on a Breakaway graphic — a button, a photo frame, a card, a little name tag — the corners are rounded, never sharp. We call this Rounded, and it is the shell every Breakaway graphic is built out of: whatever else is on the page sits inside one of these boxes. How rounded depends on what the box is — match yours to one of the five below and copy that number.

First, the words
Corner radius is how far the corner is cut into a curve, measured in pixels. Bigger number, rounder corner. In Canva it is the “corner rounding” slider; in Figma or CSS it is called radius.
What is a “container”?
Any shape that holds something else — a colored box behind text, a frame around a photo, a button behind a word. If it holds, it is a container, and it gets a rounded corner.
And a “hairline”?
A very thin border — ours is 1.5px in light grey (N300 #CFCBC9). It outlines a white card so you can see its edge without a heavy black line.

10px
Buttons & inputs
Anything you click, tap, or type into.

The full-width block at the top of a post or slide. Anything 16–20px works.
The two mistakes to avoid
1. Two roundings on one shape. Pick one number and use it on all four corners of that box.
2. Mixing sharp and round. If one card on your graphic is rounded, they all are — no square-cornered box sitting next to a rounded one.

07

The Trace

LockedPick oneYour call

Section 06 covered the rounded boxes your graphic is built out of. This section adds the one decoration allowed on top of those boxes: a red line called the Trace. It has exactly two jobs — Lead, a leading line that points at one element, or Frame, a rail that holds one block. One Trace per graphic, in one of those two modes. If you cannot name which mode you are using and what it is acting on, the graphic does not get a Trace.

The rule in one line
Rounded boxes hold your stuff. The Trace either leads to one of them or frames one of them. Those are the only two shapes we own.

Take a match-win post. The photo sits in a rounded frame, the score sits on a rounded panel, and the "WIN" label is a rounded pill. Those boxes are the shell. Then one red Trace either runs in and stops at the score (Lead) or rides the top and side of the score block to hold it together (Frame). Nothing else gets added.

No stars, arrows, swooshes, sparkles, or shapes you found in Canva. If a graphic feels empty, the fix is bigger type, a better photo, or more space. It is never another shape.

The logo is never a device, and never restyled by one. Every studio example in §08 uses one of the two modes below — nothing else is a legal Trace.
Mode A Trace · Lead

A true leading line, and the default. Enters at 45° from the nearest corner, bends once to flat, runs unbroken past the headline and stops in a hollow circle on the element it is naming. The diagonal is what makes the eye travel; the circle is what tells it where to stop.

Use it when there is exactly one focal element — a score, a date, an award, one headline. The circle must land on that element, never in empty space.
Mode B Trace · Frame

The same line used as a rail instead of a pointer. Same 45° entry, same mitered corners, but it runs the full top edge of one block and turns down once along that block's far side — no circle. It holds a block of content together instead of picking one word out of it.

Use it when the block is the message — a stat pair, a schedule panel, a quote, a sponsor row — so there is nothing single to point at. The rail must touch the block it frames on both edges it runs along; a rail floating near a block is the most common misuse.
Choosing between them — one question
Can you point at one thing and say “that, right there”?
Yes → Lead. The circle goes on that thing. Score posts, award posts, match-day dates, Reel covers.
Is the whole block the point?
Yes → Frame. The rail rides that block. Schedules, stat pairs, quotes, sponsor rows, recap captions.
Neither, or a photo already carries the frame?
Then no Trace. Rounded boxes alone. A Trace with nothing to act on is decoration, and decoration is the one thing this device is not.
Geometry · locked
Lead: 45° entry from the nearest corner → one mitered bend → flat run ≥ 1.5× the width of the element it leads to → hollow circle terminal, diameter ≈ 2.5× stroke, at the same weight.
Frame: same 45° entry → mitered bend → flat run across the full top edge of the block → one more mitered turn down the block's far side, ending flush with its bottom edge. No terminal circle.
Both are one continuous line, never broken, never rounded at the corners. Never a 90° entry, never a third bend, never two circles, never both modes on one graphic.
Stroke · pick one
Social hero 6–8px · slide 5–6px · document heading accent 2–3px. Red on light, white on dark, faint grey for background fields.
Placement · your call
Lead: flat leg about half a cap-height below the headline baseline, circle just past the last word. Frame: rail sits on the block's edges, one stroke-width outside them. Which corner the line enters from is yours in both modes.

How the class names work
Every class starts bw-. A block is the thing (bw-btn, bw-card, bw-chip); a --modifier is the variant (bw-btn--primary). Grounds are set on a wrapper — bw-on-dark or bw-on-red — and the components inside reverse themselves, including the Trace.
What it enforces for you
44px minimum hit targets, red focus rings, tabular figures in numeric cells, the five Rounded radii, the 4px spacing scale, reduced-motion and print stylesheets. The one thing it cannot check is the red budget — that is still one bw-focal per screen, and it is on you.
Not writing CSS?
Take the Tokens tab into Figma styles, a Canva brand kit, or a slide master — the names match this playbook, so “N200” and --bw-n200 are the same grey. Report anything missing rather than adding a value of your own.

When a rule and a good idea disagree
Locked rules win — take it to a mentor, and if the idea is strong the rule can change for everyone. Pick-one rules: choose again from the set. Your-call decisions never need permission. That is the whole framework: a small number of things you cannot get wrong, and a large amount of room left over.
